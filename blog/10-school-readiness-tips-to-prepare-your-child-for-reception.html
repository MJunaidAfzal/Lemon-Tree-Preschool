10 School Readiness Tips to Prepare Your Child for Reception

Forget reading and writing for a second. What actually gets tested on day one of Reception is whether your child…

Forget reading and writing for a second. What actually gets tested on day one of Reception is whether your child can get through a full day away from you, cope with a routine that isn’t theirs, and not completely unravel over something small going wrong. That’s what these school readiness tips for parents are really about: not academics, just the ordinary skills that make the first few weeks less of a shock. A handful of school readiness activities for children are in here too, nothing that needs a worksheet.

1. Run Through the Morning Routine a Few Times First

The first school morning goes badly more often than parents expect, mostly because nobody’s actually rehearsed it. A week or so before term, try getting up, dressed, fed, and out the door at roughly the real time. No pressure, no stopwatch, just so the sequence feels familiar instead of brand new.

2. Let Them Fumble Through Dressing Themselves

Zips. Buttons. Shoes that definitely go on the wrong feet half the time. It’s slower, and some mornings you’ll want to just do it yourself, but PE kits and toilet trips go a lot smoother if a four-year-old’s had practice. Easy-fasten shoes help too, at least for the first few weeks.

3. Let Lunchtime Be a Bit of a Struggle at Home First

A lunchbox a child can actually open. A banana they can peel themselves. Water poured from a small jug without you hovering. None of this is glamorous, but staff is managing thirty lunches at once, and a child who can manage their own food copes noticeably better than one who’s never had to try.

Toileting Comes Before Almost Everything Else on This List

Accidents happen, and staff deals with them without fuss; that’s not the issue. What matters more is whether a child can wipe, flush, wash their hands, and get dressed again without an adult doing it for them. If this one isn’t solid yet, it’s worth more of your attention in these last few weeks than anything else here.

4. Describe an Actual School Day, Not Just “School”

Kids don’t get anxious about school as a concept. They get anxious about the unknown parts of it. So talk through what actually happens: assembly, snack, the playground, lunch, home time in plain, boring detail. If there’s a settling-in visit or photos from the classroom, use them.

5. Practise Being Apart, Even Briefly

A few hours with grandparents. A friend’s house. A nursery session. If separation hasn’t really happened before, Reception shouldn’t be the first time it does. And don’t expect a dry-eyed goodbye on day one; that’s normal, not a sign anything’s wrong.

6. Read Together, Daily, Without Making It a Lesson

This does more for readiness than any flashcard ever will. Vocabulary, attention span, the basic sense that books are enjoyable rather than homework all of it feeds straight into how easily phonics lands once term starts. Let them turn the pages. Let them guess what happens next, even if they get it wrong.

A Round of Snap or a Jigsaw Teaches More Than It Looks Like

Sitting still. Taking turns. Losing without a meltdown. None of that needs to be called “learning”; it just needs to happen often enough that it stops being hard.

7. Give Them Two Things to Do at Once

“Pack away your toys and come sit down” is a completely normal sentence in a Reception classroom, repeated roughly a hundred times a day. If home instructions tend to be one thing at a time, two-step ones can genuinely throw a child at first. “Put your plate in the sink and grab your shoes” is an easy, low-stakes way to practise.

8. Let Them Choose, and Let Them Get It Wrong

Which toy first? What to do when the tower falls over. Small decisions, small mistakes both build the same muscle a classroom relies on constantly. It’s tempting to jump in the second something goes sideways. Try not to, when it’s safe not to.

9. Walk the Actual Route, More Than Once

Go to the open day if there is one. Beyond that, just walk or drive past the school a few times before term: the gate, the playground, wherever drop-off happens. A building that’s already familiar is one less unknown on a day that already has plenty.

10. Your Face Gives More Away Than You Think

Kids read a parent’s nerves faster than almost anything else. A quick, ordinary goodbye, even if your stomach’s in knots, tends to land better than a long, emotional one. Save that for the car.

What If My Child Still Isn’t There Yet?

There is no one date by which everything has to be sorted out. Most kids begin Reception with some things that are strong and some that are not so strong, and teachers understand this better than anyone else. If something particular is truly making you worried. Like talking, going to the bathroom, or being away from home. It’s an idea to talk to the school or your health visitor right now instead of waiting to see what happens. Early help usually works better than hoping things will get better on their own.

None of This Is a Deadline

The honest version of these school readiness tips for parents is less about ticking boxes before September and more about a few ordinary habits, built up gradually, so day one feels like a continuation of normal life rather than a cliff edge. Pick whichever one feels furthest off right now and start there.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. What are the 10 things every child needs?

Love and secure attachment, safety, a consistent routine, quality time with an adult, play, communication, healthy food and sleep, encouragement, boundaries, and the chance to build self-esteem

2. What are some tips for getting ready for school?

Practise the morning routine, build independence with dressing and toileting, talk through what a typical day looks like, and get a few short separations in beforehand so day one isn’t the very first time it’s happened.

3. What 5 skills are needed before a child is ready to read?


 Print awareness, phonological awareness, letter knowledge, listening comprehension, and motivation to read.

4. What are some activities I can do to help my child prepare for school?

Daily story time, simple jigsaws or board games, letting them dress and feed themselves, and practising two-step instructions like “put your plate away and get your shoes” all build the exact skills a Reception day relies on.

5. What are examples of school activities?

A typical day usually includes assembly or a carpet session, snack time, structured play, outdoor time, lunch, and quieter group activities like story time or craft.
