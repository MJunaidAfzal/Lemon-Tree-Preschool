Montessori-Style Learning at Lemon Tree Preschool in Manchester

Lemon Tree, a preschool in the Crumpsall and Cheetham Hill area of Manchester, follows a Montessori-style approach to early learning…

Lemon Tree, a preschool in the Crumpsall and Cheetham Hill area of Manchester, follows a Montessori-style approach to early learning. That means children largely choose their own activities from a set of purposefully designed materials, working at their own pace in a prepared environment rather than following a single fixed group lesson. For parents comparing settings across Manchester, this is often the single biggest difference to understand before booking a visit. Here’s what that looks like in practice, and what it means for your child’s day.

What Does “Montessori-Style” Actually Mean?

This method is based on being on your own and making your decisions. It is not about an adult being in charge of every activity; kids choose their own work from items that are made for a certain reason. Like sorting, pouring, beginning math, and alphabet activities. And they are allowed to spend as much time as they need on a task. The people working there act more like helpers and watchers than teachers who are in front of the room giving directions; they only step in when a child asks for help or seems ready for something new, not by controlling the speed themselves.

This does not mean the day has no structure. The items are set up carefully by type and difficulty so a child’s choices are still influenced by what’s there and what is right for their level. The freedom is in what a child takes and how long they stay with it, not in having no rules at all.

How Does This Work at Lemon Tree?

At Lemon Tree, this translates into a classroom where children move between activities they’ve chosen themselves, with staff stepping in to support, extend, or gently redirect rather than direct every moment. Group moments like circle time still happen, giving children a shared rhythm to the day and time to sing, listen to stories, and practise turn-taking together. But a significant part of the day is spent on individual or small-group work chosen by the child, which is where much of the concentration and independence-building happens.

How Does This Fit Within the EYFS?

Like every registered nursery in England, Lemon Tree works within the Early Years Foundation Stage, which sets standards for learning and development from birth to age five across seven areas of learning. This method doesn’t replace the EYFS; it’s simply the route Lemon Tree uses to deliver it, with children reaching those same developmental areas through independent, hands-on work rather than group-led lessons alone. In practice, this means the same statutory outcomes communication, physical development, early literacy and maths, and the rest are being built, just through a different route than a more traditional, teacher-led classroom would take.

What Are the Benefits of This Approach?

This style tends to click fastest with children who like sinking into one task and staying with it, rather than jumping from activity to activity. A predictable routine helps too — some children genuinely thrive on knowing what’s coming next, and working things out themselves instead of being handed the answer. Children who need more variety and constant social buzz through the day might take a bit longer to find their rhythm here, but most settle in fine once the routine and the materials become familiar.

How Do These Materials Build Independence?

Independence isn’t accidental here; it’s built into how materials and time are structured.

Self-Correcting Materials Teach Children to Learn From Mistakes

It also builds practical skills early pouring, buttoning, tidying alongside academic foundations like early letters and numbers, through materials designed to be self-correcting so children can learn from their own mistakes. A puzzle piece that only fits one way, for example, teaches a child to notice and fix an error without an adult stepping in every time.

Longer, Uninterrupted Work Periods Deepen Concentration

A day built this way is usually organised around longer, uninterrupted blocks of time rather than short activity slots. This matters because concentration builds gradually — a child who’s interrupted every ten minutes rarely gets the chance to fully absorb a task, while a longer stretch gives them room to try, get stuck, and work through it themselves.

What Should You Ask on a Visit?

Since “Montessori” isn’t a legally protected term, it’s worth asking any setting, Lemon Tree included, how the philosophy actually plays out day-to-day rather than assuming the word alone guarantees a particular style of teaching. Two nurseries can both describe themselves this way and run very different rooms.

Worth asking on a visit:

  • How much of the day is child-chosen versus adult-led?
  • What training do staff have in this specific approach?
  • How do you support a child who’s struggling to focus or choose independently?
  • How is progress tracked and shared with parents?
  • How does circle time or group activity fit alongside the independent work?

A confident, specific answer to these questions tells you far more than the label on the door.

The Bottom Line

Lemon Tree’s approach in Crumpsall and Cheetham Hill gives children in Manchester the chance to learn through independence, hands-on materials, and their own pace, within the EYFS framework every nursery follows. The clearest way to see whether it fits your child is a visit watching how they respond to choosing and focusing on their own work, and asking staff directly how a typical day actually runs.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. What are the five domains of Montessori?

Montessori children work across five areas: Life, Sensorial, Mathematics, Language and Culture.

2. What is a Montessori nursery in the UK?

A Montessori nursery in the UK is a setting built around the method. Children pick their activities from specially designed materials at their own pace, all within the standard EYFS framework.

3. What are the five principles of Montessori?

Five ideas sit at the core of Montessori: respect for the child, the absorbent mind, sensitive periods, the prepared environment, and auto‑education or learning through self‑direction.

4. What is the Montessori style of teaching? 

In the Montessori style, teachers step back into a guide’s role rather than standing at the front instructing. Montessori children choose their work from prepared materials and learn mostly through hands‑on, independent exploration.

5. What are the five senses in Montessori? 

The five senses in Montessori are sight, hearing, touch, taste, and smell. Montessori sensorial materials are built to sharpen these five senses.
